Sampling Methods

1 Sampling Methods

A sample is defined as a subset of the population. In most practical cases, the sample size \(n\) is much smaller than the population size \(N\). For instance, if \(n = 100\) and \(N = 10000\), there are about \(6.52 \times 10^{241}\) possible samples (10,000 choose 100). To this end, it is almost impossible to observe the same sample twice when we take a sample twice. In practice, however, a researcher observes only one sample out of such a large number of possible samples. Therefore, “Is my sample a good sample” is not a good question. Instead, “Is my sample obtained by a good sampling method?” is a better question to be asked. In this chapter, we discuss sampling methods (i.e., how to take a sample).

1.1 Simple Random Sampling

When all individuals in the population have the same probability of being included in a sample, it is referred to as simple random sampling (or random sampling). In other words, if the population size is \(N\) and the sample size is \(n\), simple random sampling allows a probability of \(\frac{n}{N}\) for each individual in the population to be sampled. Simple random sampling is an ideal sampling method (however it is done), but it is very challenging to execute in practice.

1.2 Stratified Sampling

Vaguely speaking, when there are two or more groups of interest and we take a simple random sample from each group, it is called stratified sampling. In the United States, the two political parties, Democratic and Republican, have different views on the same national issue. Suppose 90% of the population support the Democratic Party and 10% support the Republican Party. If a researcher takes a simple random sample of size \(n = 100\) disregarding this fact, we expect 90 individuals and 10 individuals from each party. If the researcher wants to enforce this ratio, one method of stratified sampling is to take a simple random sample of size \(n_1 = 90\) among supporters of the Democratic Party and a simple random sample of size \(n_2 = 10\) among supporters of the Republican Party. If the researcher wants to balance the two groups for some reason, one method of stratified sampling is to take a simple random sample of size \(n_1 = 50\) and a simple random sample of size \(n_2 = 50\) from each party. As such, when there are \(g\) groups of interest and we take a simple random sample of size \(n_i\) from each group such that \(n_1 + n_2 + \dots + n_g = n\) is fixed, it is referred to as stratified sampling.

1.3 Cluster Sampling

When a researcher randomly selects groups and observes all individuals in the selected groups, it is referred to as cluster sampling. It is a useful strategy to obtain a large sample size. Here is an example of education research. Suppose there are 12 classrooms and there are 30 students per classroom. It is easier to observe all students in four classrooms (a total of 120 students) rather than observing 120 students randomly selected across the 12 classrooms. A researcher may randomly select four classrooms and observe all students in the four selected classrooms. In this case, each classroom is a cluster, and there are a total of 120 students in four clusters (30 students per cluster).

1.4 Convenience Sampling

Broadly speaking, when a researcher includes easily accessible individuals in a sample, it is referred to as convenience sampling. It is not recommended, but it is often done in practice. Researchers should be aware of potential source of bias in their findings based on a convenient sample. For example, suppose the goal is to estimate the population average SAT score in mathematics (parameter). If a statistics professor takes a sample of students in the Department of Mathematics and Statistics because they are easily accessible, it is likely to over-estimate the parameter.

1.5 Voluntary Sampling

In human subject research, there must be an informed consent by participants, and all human subjects have the right not to participate in the research. Therefore, we can only observe human subjects who volunteered to be in the sample, and it is referred to as voluntary sampling . For example, suppose the goal is to estimate the proportion of college students who uses illegal drugs on a regular basis (parameter). If drug users do not participate in the survey (even though their identity will be protected) or drug users lie in the survey (because they are afraid of revealing illegal actions), it is likely to under-estimate the parameter. As such, studying human subjects are challenging in various aspects.
