Conditions and Post-Hoc Tests for ANOVA

1 Conditions for ANOVA

As in the two-sample t-test, the ANOVA requires two conditions be met in order to trust the results of the test.

1.1 Normality Condition

  • Let \(\mu_j\) and \(\sigma_j\) be the population average and standard deviation of Population \(j\) for \(j = 1, 2, \dots, g\). Assume that a simple random sample is taken from each population which follows a normal distribution \(N(\mu, \sigma_i)\), and it is referred to as the normality assumption. Further assume that \(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \dots = \sigma_g^2\), it is referred to as the equal variance assumption, and we simply denote the equal variance by \(\sigma^2\). The normality and equal variance condition are graphically shown below for \(g = 3\) groups.

2 Case Study: Exam Scores and Seats

2.1 Hypotheses

Let \(\mu_1\), \(\mu_2\), and \(\mu_3\) be the unknown expected eaxm score for \(\texttt{Front}\), \(\texttt{Middle}\), and \(\texttt{Back}\), respectively. Our goal is to test for the null hypothesis \(H_0\): \(\mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu_3\) (i.e., the expected exam score is the same between the three seating rows) versus the alternative hypothesis is \(H_1\): \(\mu_1 \ne \mu_2\), \(\mu_1 \ne \mu_3\), or \(\mu_2 \ne \mu_3\) (i.e., at least one seating row has a different expected exam score) at a significance level of \(\alpha = 0.05\).

2.2 ANOVA

The sample sizes are \(n_1 = 7\), \(n_2 = 8\), and \(n_3 = 6\) for \(\texttt{Front}\), \(\texttt{Middle}\), and \(\texttt{Back}\), respectively. The sample means are \(\bar{X}_1 = 89.29\), \(\bar{X}_2 = 81.75\), and \(\bar{X}_3 = 77.83\), and the sample standard deviations are \(S_1 = 6.626\), \(S_2 = 7.686\), and \(S_3 = 8.909\). It appears that students in \(\texttt{Middle}\) scored slightly higher than in \(\texttt{Back}\) by 3.92 points on average, and students in \(\texttt{Front}\) scored substantially higher than in \(\texttt{Middle}\) by 7.54 points on average. The variance of exam scores by are similar among the three groups (sample standard deviations are similar).

2.3 Equal Variance Assumption

The population variances \(\sigma_1^2\), \(\sigma_2^2\), and \(\sigma_3^2\) can be estimated by the sample variances \(S_1^2\), \(S_2^2\), and \(S_3^2\), respectively. As a simple rule of thumb of checking equal variance assumption, if the largest sample variance is not greater than four times of the smallest sample variance, it is considered as no strong sign of violating equal variance assumption. In this sample, \(\frac{(8.9088)^2}{(6.6261)^2} = 1.8\), so the equal variance assumption is good.

As in the two-sample t-test, it can be formally assessed by the Bartlett test.

bartlett.test(score ~ seat, data = examscore)$p.value
[1] 0.7999561

The Bartlett test results in p = 0.8 (greater than \(\alpha = 0.05\)), so there is no sign of violating the equal variance assumption.

Since both normality assumption and equal variance assumptions are satisfied, the conclusion of \(H_1\) in the ANOVA test is valid.

For the purpose of demonstration, suppose we want to compare three averages \(\mu_1\), \(\mu_2\), and \(\mu_3\). The conclusion of ANOVA is either \(H_0\): \(\mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu_3\) or \(H_1\): \(\mu_1 \ne \mu_2\), \(\mu_1 \ne \mu_3\), or \(\mu_2 \ne \mu_3\). If \(H_1\) is concluded at a significance level of \(\alpha\), it does not specify which pairwise difference(s) are statistically significant. The Tukey’s method (also known as the Tukey’s honest significant differences) is a post-hoc test (identifying significant pairwise difference(s)) when the conclusion of ANOVA is \(H_1\).

3 Family-wise Confidence Level

A general form of a confidence interval (CI) for any unknown parameter is

\[ \texttt{point} \,\, \texttt{estimate} \,\, \pm \,\, c \times \texttt{SE} \tag{1}\]

where \(c\) is the critical value which reflects a confidence level \(1 - \alpha\) and \(\texttt{SE}\) is the standard error which quantifies the uncertainty about point estimation. If a CI for \(\mu_j - \mu_k\) does not include zero at a confidence level of \(1 - \alpha\), it is equivalent to concluding \(\mu_j - \mu_k \ne 0\) (a two-sided test) at a significance level of \(\alpha\). It is a natural idea to build three 95% CIs, one for \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\), one for \(\mu_1 - \mu_3\), and one for \(\mu_2 - \mu_3\), and determine which pairwise difference(s) are statistically significant.

Here is a challenge. When there are three independent CIs for \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\), \(\mu_1 - \mu_3\), and \(\mu_2 - \mu_3\), where each CI captures the true value of \(\mu_j - \mu_k\) with a probability of 0.95, all three independent CIs capture the true values of \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\), \(\mu_1 - \mu_3\), and \(\mu_2 - \mu_3\) with a probability of \((0.95)^3 = 0.857\). In this case, we say that we have a family-wise confidence level of 0.857. In general, a family-wise confidence level is the probability that all CIs capture the true parameter values. If we want a family-wise confidence level of 0.95, the confidence level for each individual CI should be \((0.95)^{1/3} = 0.983\). In general, when we want \(k\) independent CIs at a family-wise confidence level of \(1 - \alpha\), each confidence interval should have a confidence level of \((1 - \alpha)^{1/k}\). This method is known as the Sidak’s method.

This method is brilliant, but there are two caveats. The first caveat is that CIs for multiple pairwise differences are not independent. The second caveat, which follows from the first caveat, is that Sidak’s method is conservative. In other words, it results in long CIs (imprecise interval estimation).

4 Tukey’s Honestly Significance Difference (HSD)

John Tukey (1915-2000), an American mathematician and statistician, suggested a method of accurately calculating the critical value \(c\) and the standard error \(\texttt{SE}\) in Equation 1. The formula of \(c\) is complicated, but \(c\) can be calculated by \(\texttt{qtukey}( 1 - \alpha, \, \texttt{nmeans} = g, \, \texttt{df} = \nu_w ) \, / \, \texttt{sqrt}(2)\) where \(1 - \alpha\) is a fixed family-wise confidence level, \(g\) is the number of groups, and \(\nu_w = m - g\) is the within-group degrees of freedom (\(m\) is the total sample size). The standard error is estimated as

\[ \texttt{SE} = \sqrt{ \texttt{MSW} \left( \frac{1}{n_j} + \frac{1}{n_k} \right) } \]

where \(\texttt{MSW}\) is the within-group mean square (from the ANOVA table) and \(n_j\) and \(n_k\) are the sample sizes in group \(j\) and \(k\), respectively. This method is known as the Tukey’s method or Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD).

Note: As the ANOVA test, the Tukey’s HSD is valid under the normality and equal variance assumptions.

Let us apply the Tukey’s HSD to \(\texttt{seat.csv}\). Let \(\mu_1\), \(\mu_2\), and \(\mu_3\) be the unknown expected exam score for \(\texttt{Front}\), \(\texttt{Middle}\), and \(\texttt{Back}\), respectively. Below are the descriptive statistics, ANOVA table, and critical value.

At a family-wise confidence level of 0.95, Tukey’s CIs for \(\mu_2 - \mu_1\), \(\mu_3 - \mu_1\), and \(\mu_3 - \mu_2\) can be calculated as follows:

  • CI for \(\mu_2 - \mu_1\): \((81.750 - 89.286) \, \pm \, 2.552 \sqrt{59.653 \left( \frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{7} \right) }\)
